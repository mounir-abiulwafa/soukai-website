Is This USPS Text a Scam? How to Check Before You Click

A package text can look urgent when it says a delivery failed or a small fee is due. Treat the message as unverified until you check the delivery through an official USPS channel you open yourself.

Quick answer

A USPS text may be a scam when it pressures you to click a delivery link, confirm an address, or pay a small redelivery fee. A real-looking name or package reference is not enough to prove a text is genuine. Do not use the link in the message; check any delivery independently through USPS.

Why fake USPS texts work

Package delivery scams often arrive when people are expecting an order. They may claim a delivery failed, an address needs confirmation, or a parcel will be returned unless you act quickly. The requested amount can be very small because the goal may be to collect card details or lead you to a fake sign-in page.

Fictional example“USPS: We could not deliver your parcel. Confirm your address and pay a $0.30 redelivery fee at usps-delivery-update.example before 6 PM.” This is fictional. The example domain is not live. The deadline, fee, and unfamiliar domain are reasons to verify independently rather than click.

Red flags in a USPS scam text

  • A demand to pay a small fee for redelivery, customs, or address confirmation.
  • Urgency such as “final notice,” “expires today,” or a threat that your package will be returned.
  • A suspicious delivery link with extra words, misspellings, or an unfamiliar domain.
  • A request for card details, an account password, a one-time code, or other personal information.
  • A tracking number or sender name that cannot be matched to an order you made.
  • A text that asks you to reply, call a number in the message, or move quickly before you can verify it.

What USPS normally does versus suspicious behavior

Delivery communications can vary, so no single sign proves a message is fake. The safer pattern is to use the tracking details from your order confirmation or enter the tracking number on an official USPS page you reached yourself. A message should not prevent you from taking a moment to verify through that independent route.

How to check a USPS tracking text safely

  1. Do not tap the text link, reply, or call a number supplied in the message.
  2. Open a new browser window or the official USPS app yourself.
  3. Use a tracking number from your original order confirmation, not one supplied only by the text.
  4. Compare the status with the seller’s order page or contact the seller through a known support channel.
  5. If a payment is requested, verify the need directly with the delivery service before entering card information anywhere.

If you already clicked

Close the page without entering anything or downloading a file. If you entered a password, change it on the real service and anywhere else you reused it. You can also learn how to assess a suspicious link before opening similar messages in the future.

If you entered card or payment information

Contact your card issuer or payment provider promptly using a trusted number or app, explain what happened, and ask about available protective steps. Review recent activity, follow the provider’s instructions, and be cautious of follow-up messages that claim to help resolve the issue.

USPS text scam FAQ

Can a fake USPS text use my name or order details?

It can use details from a data breach, a public profile, or a guess. Personal-looking information is a reason to verify more carefully, not proof that a text is real.

Is a USPS tracking text with a link always a scam?

No. But the safest approach is the same: do not rely on the text link. Open a known official channel yourself and check there.

Should I report a suspicious package delivery scam?

Keep a record of the text and use the reporting options offered by your carrier, phone provider, card issuer, or relevant consumer-protection agency where appropriate.
